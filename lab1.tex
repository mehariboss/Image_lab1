\subsection{Signal Generation and Visualization}
1 (a)
The sawtooth signal can be mathematically represented by the function:
\[
x(t) = 2((ft + 0.5) \bmod 1) - 1.
\]
Multiplying \(f\) by \(t\) gives us how many cycles have passed at time \(t\). Adding 0.5 shifts the signal by half a period so that it is centered around \(t\) = 0. The modulo ensures the value within the interval [0, 1) and makes the signal restart after each period. Then multiplying by 2 and subtracting 1 scales the signal to the range [-1, 1).
\smallbreak
1 (b) The frequency \(f\) is defined as the number of oscillations per unit time. When the frequency is doubled from 10 to 20 Hz, each cycle takes half as much time, which makes the signal appear more compressed in the time direction.
\smallbreak
1 (c) Modifying the square wave still alternates between -1 and 1, but unlike before it starts at -1. This is because \(np.ceil(0)\) is 0, and after multiplying by 2 and subtracting 1, the result becomes -1 and positive sine values returns 1.
\smallbreak
1 (d) The composite signal consists is the sum of the sine wave and the square wave. When the square wave is at +1, the sine wave is shifted up, and the square wave is at -1, it is shifted down. This results in a signal that oscillates between -2 and 2.

1 (e) It can help to determine how a signal should be processed. For example, in audio processing, an equalizer can increase or decrease different frequency ranges such as bass, midrange and treble.

\subsection{Sampling and Aliasing}
3(a) The Nyquist rate is 20 Hz and 15 Hz is not sufficent enough.
\smallbreak
3(b) Increasing the sampling rate to 25 Hz gives it more samples to be above the Nyquist rate of 20 Hz, so the values contain enough to represent and reconstruct the original signal.
\smallbreak
3(c) The signal is sampled at 30 Hz, which is below the Nyquist rate of 40 Hz. Therefore aliasing occurs. The 20 Hz signal appears as a lower frequency component at 10 Hz because frequencies above half the sampling rate are folded into lower frequency range.
\smallbreak
3(d) The original signal has a frequency of 25 Hz and it's being sampled at 12 Hz. Because the sampling rate is lower then the expected Nyquist rate, aliasing occurs. This makes the signal appear to have frequency at 1 Hz, even though the orignal signal has 25 cycles per second.
\smallbreak
3 (e) A scenario where aliasing is important is when an audio signal is sampled at a rate that is too low. High frequency components can then be reconstructed as incorrect lower frequency. Therefore, the sampling rate must be above the Nyquist rate in order correctly capture the highest frequency in the signal.

5 (a) According to the PSNR values, the resampled image gave the lowest score with 22.71 dB, compared to 29.21 dB for the requantized image. A lower PSNR means the image is further from the original, so resampling introduces more distortion.

5 (d) PSNR is simple to calculate, has a clear physical meaning and is mathematically conventient, but it does match the perceived visual quality very well. SSIM instead compares three things between the original image, luminance, contrast and structure. This is based on the idea that the human visual system is adapted to extact structural information, which PSNR does not take into account \cite{wangImageQualityAssessment2004}
\smallbreak
5 (e) Both metrics agrees that requantization gives better results. The PSNR is 29.21 dB for requantization and 22.71 dB for resampling, and the SSIM is 0.88 for requantization and 0.66 for resampled, where 1 means identical to the original image. PSNR compares the images pixel by pixel, which gives a simple mathematical measure. SSIM instead compares the structure of the images, and the values show that the resampled image loses its structure because the edges become blocky, while the reqauntized keeps its structure but has fewer grey levels.
